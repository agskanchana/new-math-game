Why about 350?

A school year has about 36 weeks. A lesson takes about three short days (new, practised, quick), and reviews come back later, so a child can learn at most 50 to 60 lessons a year. Little ones learn fewer, bigger steps: 25 at age 3, up to 54 at age 9 (Grade 4).

One lesson, from new to learned

A lesson is played in more than one activity, so children see the same idea in different ways. Stars count first tries only.

Learn it

Day 1. The friend shows the idea in the main activity, then 5 rounds with lots of help (the empty windows glow, the plank tips live).

Another way

Day 2. The same lesson in a second activity: the ten-frame in Poke Pals becomes two quick-look frames in Peek-a-Peep.

Quick round

From age 6. The same lesson at speed: ten in a row against your best time (see below).

Comes back

In the Hatch (the daily ten questions) and in reviews after a few days, a week and a month.

Stars and help

  • 5 of 5 right on the first try = 3 stars, 4 = 2, 3 = 1.
  • A miss is never "wrong": the friend gives a tip; after a second miss it shows the working step by step, then a fresh question of the same kind.
  • Every round can be a story: who wants what, and why.
  • If a lesson stays hard, the road brings an easier lesson of the same topic first.

Speed: quick to finish, from age 6

Every activity has a quick round for ages 6 and up

  • The same game, the building done for you: the Peeps are already on board, the bed is already drawn, the clock already shows the time. The child only answers, on the number pad or with one tap. A question takes seconds, not a minute.
  • Short: ten questions at 6 and 7, a 60-second round from 8. About a minute.
  • Your time and your best are always on screen; a run of right answers lights a streak; beating your best earns a crown and a "new best" cheer. A small chart shows the last rounds getting quicker.
  • Never blocked: animations are short and the next question comes at once; the end show can be skipped.
  • Right before quick: only right answers count. A miss shows the answer at once and the question comes back later.
  • Racing yourself only: no lists of other children's times.
  • Under 6: no clocks. The calm, hands-on game; no hurry.
  • Where: the warm-up (Volcano Pop or Kart Cup, taking turns, with the current lessons' facts) and the practise stop are quick rounds; a new lesson is always learned calmly first.

From Grade 3: practice, not games

Up to age 7 children learn by playing. From Grade 3 (age 8) the lessons are calm practice screens: one clean Clay Bay screen for each kind of question, the maths in the middle, a friend at the side, the number pad. Older children get on with the maths, and many more kinds of question can be made quickly.

1See it

Times is equal groups, age 8: three groups of four Peeps; one tap answers 12.

2Fill one part

Multiply by one digit, age 9: 23 × 4 split into 20 and 3; type 3 × 4.

3Do it all

Fractions on a number line, age 8: where is the flag? Type 3/4.

4Word problem

Take away decimals, age 10: Digsy's rope, with a bar to help.

5Quick

Times 6 and 7, age 8: ten in a row against your best, with a streak.

Every skill climbs the same steps

  1. See it with a model: Peeps in groups, an area model, a number line, a bar.
  2. Fill one part of the working.
  3. Do it all: the bare question.
  4. Check it: guess first, spot the mistake.
  5. Word problems: the friends' own stories.
  6. Quick: the same questions at speed, against your best.

What stays fun

  • Stars, coins and the dress-up, and the Play Park, opened with coins from lessons.
  • The Hatch: ten questions a day, and the egg hatches.
  • The quick facts games (Volcano Pop and Kart Cup, taking turns) as the warm-up, racing your best.
  • The friends on every screen, with tips, and never "wrong".

How an activity changes with the lesson and the age

Each activity has a few settings. A lesson is a "content card" that sets them; nothing else changes. For example, Seesaw Twins:

Numbers

pictures at 3, numbers to 10 at 5, to 20 at 6, to 100 at 7

What you see

things, then numbers, then number sentences; the plank always tips live

Help

counted aloud, glowing places, a hint after a miss, the working after two

How you answer

hands-on (drag, pour, pull, press) to learn; the number pad or one tap to be quick

The clock

none under 6; ten in a row against your best at 6 and 7

The story

who asks for what: Pom's order, the twins' fair share, Digsy's customer

A day: one road, picked from the map

  1. Warm-up (2 minutes): quick facts from the current lessons. Volcano Pop and Kart Cup take turns from age 5; Peek-a-Peep at 3-4.
  2. Review: an older lesson that is due again, in a different activity.
  3. New: the next lesson on the map, learned calmly.
  4. Practise: yesterday's lesson in its second activity, as a quick round from 6.
  5. The Hatch: ten mixed questions from the week's lessons; the egg hatches.

About 10 to 15 minutes. The road chooses the lessons; a child can also open My maths and play any lesson already reached. From age 8, review, new and practise are practice screens; the warm-up and the Hatch stay the same.

Just for fun: the Play Park

Some games have nothing to do with maths or English: they are simply fun. Children open them with coins they earn by doing lessons, so the fun is the reward for learning.

How coins work

  • Earned only by learning: every stop of the day's road 5 coins, three stars +5, a new best in a quick round +5, the Hatch 15, the whole road +10: about 50 coins on a good day, from any subject (maths now, English and reading later).
  • Spent on fun: a game in the Play Park costs 5 to 10 coins a play (a few minutes); clothes in the dress-up 10 to 40.
  • Never sold: coins cannot be bought with money.
  • Grown-ups decide how much play a day, and whether the Play Park opens only after the day's road.
  • Clay Bay through and through: the fun games use the same clay world and friends (Peeps, Fizz, the critters…), with no questions in them.

Which school list

This plan uses the US list (Kindergarten to Grade 5; its codes such as K.OA.4 or 3.NF.2 are on every lesson) with early-years goals for ages 3 and 4, because it is clear and covers ages 5 to 10 step by step. Other lists (UK, Singapore) teach almost the same lessons in a slightly different order: the lessons stay, only their order, their age and their codes change. Settings follow the family's country: coins and notes; centimetres or inches.

Questions

  1. The structure: 8 ages → 10 topics → lessons → 17 games (ages 3 to 7) and practice screens (8 to 10). Does it work for you? Are the topic names right?
  2. About 344 lessons (from 25 at age 3 to 54 at Grade 4): too many, too few, about right?
  3. The school list: start with the US list, or the client's own country's list? Which country?
  4. Speed from age 6: quick rounds against your own best, as drawn. Enough, or also class or friends' leaderboards?
  5. The nine new games from your picks (Kart Cup, Peep Parachutes, Island Hopper, Marble Run, Raft Rescue, Parcel Post, Chocolate Snap, Mirror Wings, Boat Load): anything to change in their storyboards? Say the game and the frame number.
  6. From Grade 3 (age 8): practice screens instead of games, as drawn? May older children still open the younger games, just for fun?
  7. My maths: may children pick any lesson they have reached, or only follow the day's road?
  8. Coins and units: which country's coins, and centimetres, inches or both?
  9. Grown-ups: what should parents (or teachers) see and be able to change?
  10. The Play Park: which fun games would you like, and how many coins should a play cost?
  11. Next step: build the lessons as data and one activity across all its ages first (for example Poke Pals, ages 3 to 7), then the rest?
