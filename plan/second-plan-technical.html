Paper Town: the technical side

How the paper characters move, how paper is drawn on screen, the timings, a tap-only way for every paper move, and the risks. The tools are the same as the first plan's (its technical page: Rive with an animator, or our own code rig).

1 · Characters

Paper puppets and folded hinges

The doll is a classic paper puppet: flat parts held by split pins. Each friend is a real origami model: an artist folds it first, so its crease pattern is real, and the app bends it along those creases. A whale and an octopus do not borrow a human skeleton; each animal has its own small set of hinges.

2 · Paper on screen

Four layers, one texture

Every screen is the scene (a Gemini-style painting, made once), the characters (rigged), the maths pieces (tickets, strips, grids, mats, dots: drawn by code, exact, so a test robot can count them) and the buttons. All paper shares one small fibre texture and one kind of soft shadow, which keeps it light. Depth lives in the scenery; the maths is always flat and straight-on.

A round's reward and the move to the next part take 2 seconds at most together. Taps during a movement are kept and played in order. Sounds: a soft rustle, a short rip along a perforation, a blunt scissor click, a wooden stamp thump, the clack of a hole punch, a string pluck, cardboard drums. A thinking tip never has a failure sound.

5 · Sizes

Reading and touching

At least
Reading text22 px
Main maths (numbers, signs)40 px
Headings32 px
Ordinary buttons48 × 48 px
Main buttons for ages 3–464 × 64 px

Headings in Bricolage Grotesque, reading in Atkinson Hyperlegible, digits in Lexend (its 0 has no slash, as in school books; all free, OFL). Ink on warm paper has a contrast of about 12 to 1; warm paper on deep teal about 7 to 1. Instructions are always spoken and pictured: a 3-year-old never needs to read.

6 · Risks

What could go wrong, and the fix

RiskFix
3- and 4-year-olds cannot tear, fold or cut reliably with a fingerThey start with stamping, opening and placing. Main buttons 64 × 64 px. Every hard gesture has a tap-only way. A slipped gesture never uses up a try.
A helpful "snap" gives the answer awayPieces snap only to the crease, perforation or place the child chose, and a wrong choice stays wrong.
Left-handed children cover the workTrays and buttons move to the other side; labels sit above the hand; number lines and clocks keep their direction.
Small phones get crowdedOne task at a time, choices below the work, five Proof Press panels at a time for the youngest, a full-screen view for folding and fractions.
Paper texture makes numbers hard to readNumbers and signs sit on plain warm paper; no fibre texture behind them.
Colours start to mean numbersPaper colour never follows the amount; labels or patterns when colour matters.
Music distracts or excludesSeparate switches for voice, music and sounds; captions and a soft visual beat; a muted band follows the same rules.
Tempo makes children guessNo timed play under 8; slow right answers are fine; bests need accuracy; the untimed version earns the same.
The town grows too big for memoryOne shared paper texture, only the current activity loaded, at most 12 town pieces on screen at once.
Origami is costly to animateReal folded models first, crease hinges, small movements; unfolding into the square is an extra, not every move.
Perspective bends the mathsAreas, fractions, shadows and measuring are flat drawings; depth is only in the scenery.
The day's story forces the mathsSkills are picked first, then an event is fitted around them.
The paper look resembles a known gameOur own animal shapes, ink faces and craft-table town; no envelope-headed people, no copied costumes or folded companions.
A happy finale hides a struggleParents' records keep first tries, corrected tries and twins apart; finishing never means learned.
